
# Proof 78: Early Christian Art Confirms This

## The Claim

The earliest Christian art — from Roman catacombs, house churches, and early basilicas — consistently depicts biblical figures as Europeans. This art predates political motivations and reflects genuine early Christian understanding.

---

## The Catacombs of Rome

### Dating

Roman catacombs contain art from:
- ~2nd century AD (earliest)
- Through ~4th century AD
- Before Christianity became state religion
- Created by believers, for believers

### What They Show

| Subject | Depiction |
|---------|-----------|
| Christ | Young shepherd, Roman appearance |
| Moses | Roman figure, European |
| Jonah | European youth |
| Daniel | Roman/European |
| The Three Hebrews | European appearance |
| Apostles | Roman men |

All biblical figures depicted as Europeans.

---

## Dura-Europos

### The Synagogue (~244 AD)

A synagogue in Syria with painted walls:
- Moses depicted with European features
- Biblical scenes in Greco-Roman style
- Light skin, varied appearances
- Destroyed ~256 AD, preserved under sand

### The House Church (~235 AD)

The earliest known house church:
- Christ depicted healing
- European appearance
- Greco-Roman artistic style

---

## Pre-Constantinian Art

### Before 313 AD

Art from before Constantine's legalization of Christianity:
- No political motivation
- Created by persecuted believers
- Represents genuine understanding
- Consistently European depiction

### Why This Matters

This art was not:
- State propaganda
- Politically motivated
- Created for public consumption
- Designed to please rulers

It was authentic Christian self-expression.

---

## Common Scenes

### Good Shepherd

The most common early image:
- Christ as young shepherd
- Carrying a sheep
- Roman pastoral dress
- European features

### Orant Figures

Praying figures with raised hands:
- Men and women
- Roman dress
- European appearance
- Representing believers

### Jonah Cycle

The story of Jonah:
- Common in catacomb art
- Jonah as European youth
- Ship, whale, gourd — Greco-Roman style

### Daniel and Lions

Daniel in the lions' den:
- Young European man
- Nude or lightly clothed
- Roman artistic conventions

## Sarcophagi

### Christian Burial Art

Early Christian sarcophagi show:
- Biblical scenes
- Christ and apostles
- Old Testament figures
- All with European features

### Junius Bassus Sarcophagus (~359 AD)

One of the finest early Christian sarcophagi:
- Christ depicted young
- European features
- Apostles as Romans
- Biblical scenes with Roman figures

---

## Mosaic Evidence

### Santa Maria Maggiore (~430s AD)

Early basilica mosaics:
- Abraham, Isaac, Jacob — European
- Moses, Joshua — European
- Christ — European
- Consistent depiction

### Ravenna Mosaics (~5th-6th century)

San Vitale, Sant'Apollinare:
- Christ in glory — European
- Apostles — European
- Old Testament figures — European

---

## No Diversity in Early Art

### What's Absent

Early Christian art lacks:
- Racial diversity in biblical figures
- "Middle Eastern" appearance
- Black Christ or apostles
- Any distinction from Roman/European appearance

### The Implication

Early Christians:
- Didn't see biblical figures as racially different
- Depicted them as they saw themselves
- Made no effort to "exoticize" them

Because there was no difference to depict.

---

## Artistic Continuity

### From Catacombs to Cathedrals

| Era | Depiction |
|-----|-----------|
| 2nd-3rd century | European (catacombs) |
| 4th-5th century | European (basilicas) |
| 6th-10th century | European (Byzantine) |
| 11th-15th century | European (Medieval) |
| 16th-18th century | European (Renaissance/Baroque) |

Unbroken tradition of European depiction.

---

## Synthesis

Early Christian art confirms:

1. **Earliest evidence** — ~2nd century, European depiction
2. **Pre-Constantine** — No political motivation
3. **Multiple locations** — Rome, Syria, everywhere
4. **Consistent** — All European features
5. **Continuous** — Unchanged for centuries

The earliest Christians depicted biblical figures as European because they believed — and knew — they were.
